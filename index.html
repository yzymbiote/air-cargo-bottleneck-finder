How this works

The problem. Export cargo has to pass four stages before a flight's cutoff. If one stage can't keep up, especially in the rush before a cutoff, shipments pile up there and miss their flight. Adding resources in the wrong place costs money without fixing anything.

The method: discrete-event simulation. The tool creates a realistic day of shipments. Arrival times are random, averaging your hourly rates, and processing times vary randomly around your averages. It then follows every shipment through each stage. If all staff or machines at a stage are busy, the shipment waits in line. Priority and perishable shipments go to the front of each line.

Repeatable randomness. One random day can be lucky or unlucky, so the tool simulates many days and averages them. A fixed "seed" makes the random numbers repeatable, so you always get the same answer for the same inputs. When comparing options, every option is tested on the same simulated days, so any difference comes from the change itself, not from luck.

What the measures mean.

  • Average wait: minutes a shipment queues at a stage before someone starts on it.
  • Average queue: how many shipments are waiting at that stage, averaged over the day.
  • Utilisation: share of the day the staff or machines are busy. Above about 85% queues grow quickly.
  • Bottleneck: the stage where shipments wait longest. This is the constraint that limits the whole terminal.
  • Missed cutoff: a shipment booked on today's flight that has not finished ramp transfer by that flight's cutoff.

Finding a solution. With 4 stages and a budget of 3, there are 35 ways to add up to 3 resources, so the tool simply tests them all (an exhaustive search) instead of guessing. It ranks options by missed cutoffs first, then by fewest extra resources, then by shortest time through the terminal. Adding resources only to the bottleneck often just moves the queue to the next stage, which is why the best answer is frequently a mix.

Key assumptions (simplifications).

  • Each shipment is handled one at a time at each stage. Real build-up and ramp work is often done in batches per flight.
  • Processing times range from 0.5× to 1.7× the average. Most are slightly quicker than average and a few take much longer.
  • Staff and machines are available all day at the numbers entered, with no breaks, shifts or breakdowns.
  • Every shipment passes screening. There are no rejections, re-checks or documentation holds.
  • Work stops at closing time. Anything unfinished is counted as still in the terminal.
